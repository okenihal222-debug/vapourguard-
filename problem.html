The Problem

Pump attendants: chronic, everyday exposure

Benzene is a volatile component of petrol vapor and a recognised human carcinogen. Attendants are exposed twice over: at the nozzle during every single refuelling, and near the tank vents whenever a tanker unloads — often across 8–12 hour shifts, standing directly at the point of release.

Multiple peer-reviewed Indian studies have compared long-serving pump attendants against unexposed control groups and found measurably altered blood and hematology markers — a biological signature consistent with chronic low-level benzene exposure, not just occupational discomfort. VERIFIED — published studies

Small and rural stations are exactly where this risk is least mitigated: they're the segment without Stage 2 nozzle-recovery hardware, so attendants there breathe vapor that urban pump workers largely no longer do.

This is the exposure VaporGuard's Stage 2 vapor-assist nozzle line is built to close — capturing vapor at the same point attendants stand, instead of letting it disperse into the air they breathe all shift.

The Problem

Station owners: fuel they already paid for, gone

Evaporative loss isn't a rounding error — industry technical literature puts it at roughly 0.18%–0.5% of total throughput across both capture stages, and about two-thirds of that happens during tank refilling (Stage 1) alone, before a single vehicle is even served. VERIFIED — industry literature

The owner has already paid the dealer/OMC price for every litre in that tank. Fuel lost to evaporation isn't insured, isn't reimbursed, and isn't recoverable after the fact — it's a straight subtraction from margin on a business that already runs on thin per-litre commissions.

Because the loss is invisible (there's no meter that shows "litres evaporated today"), most small owners have never had a number to attach to it — which is exactly the gap VaporGuard's dashboard is built to close.

The Problem

Nearby communities: air quality at ground level

Fuel vapor released during refuelling and tank-fill events isn't only a worker-exposure issue — it's released at ground level, right where people walk, shop, and live, especially in the dense small-town markets where many rural and semi-urban stations sit close to residential and commercial buildings.

VOCs from uncontrolled fuel handling contribute to local air-quality degradation and, in the presence of sunlight and other pollutants, can react to form ground-level ozone.

The scale of what's achievable is already demonstrated: cities where vapor recovery has been adopted have seen VOC emissions cut by roughly 84%. VERIFIED — market research, 2025 That improvement has so far been concentrated in urban centers — rural and small-town clusters, nearly 29% of India's station network, haven't seen the same benefit.

Closing that gap at the community level is a direct side-effect of closing it at the individual-station level — every station that adopts capture technology contributes to the same direction of improvement already proven in cities.

Our Solution

Why combining both stages into one retrofit matters

Existing vapor recovery systems typically treat Stage 1 (tank-fill capture) and Stage 2 (nozzle-refuelling capture) as two separate large installations — each with its own footprint, plumbing, and installation cost. That's manageable for a high-throughput city station with space and capital to spare; it's a non-starter for a small rural outlet.

VaporGuard's retrofit unit integrates both capture stages into a single compact enclosure, sharing sensors, valves, and the monitoring controller between them rather than duplicating hardware. That's the core design decision that makes a small-station price point possible at all.

Practically, this also means one installation visit, one enclosure to maintain, and one dashboard — not two separate systems an owner has to think about.

Our Solution

How the anomaly detection actually works

The edge AI model doesn't start with a fixed rulebook — it baselines each station's own normal recovery pattern over its first weeks of operation: how much vapor a typical tank-fill or refuelling event returns, at what pressure, over what time window.

Once that baseline exists, the model watches for deviations — a coupling that suddenly returns less vapor than its own history predicts is a strong, early signal of a worn seal or a developing leak, well before it's large enough for a person to notice or smell.

Because inference runs on-device rather than depending on a constant cloud connection, the fault-detection function keeps working even during the patchy connectivity many rural sites actually have — a deliberate design choice, not an afterthought.

When an anomaly is flagged, it's surfaced two ways: instantly through the enclosure's status LED, and remotely through an SMS/app alert to the owner's phone — see the Live Dashboard for what that alert stream looks like.

Why we lead with rupees, not compliance

Where the savings actually come from

Benefits — Customer

Why early fault alerts protect uptime

A small station typically runs on one or two dispensers — there's no redundancy. An unplanned fault that takes a dispenser offline mid-day is a direct, immediate hit to that day's revenue, not just a maintenance inconvenience.

Because the AI layer flags a developing fault (a worn seal, a pressure anomaly) before it becomes a hard failure, an owner can schedule the fix during a genuinely low-traffic window — early morning, late night — instead of losing peak-hour throughput to an emergency repair.

This is the same underlying anomaly-detection mechanism described in the Solution section above, just viewed from the owner's operational side rather than the technical side.

Benefits — Customer

Safety is more than the headline health case

Lower vapor concentration at the nozzle and tank vents reduces attendant benzene exposure, as covered in the Problem section above — but reduced fuel-vapor concentration in the air around a dispensing point also lowers the immediate fire and flashpoint risk that comes with any accumulation of flammable vapor in a workspace.

This is a basic workplace-safety expectation at any modern station, and one that small/rural sites currently have less engineering support to meet than their urban counterparts, who've had Stage 2 systems for longer.

Benefits — Environmental

Getting ahead of a mandate that may expand

The December 2021 NGT directive currently requires vapor recovery only for new petroleum outlets — existing stations, including nearly all rural ones, face no mandate today. But the regulatory direction over the past several years has consistently moved toward wider VRS coverage, not narrower.

A station that installs VaporGuard now isn't reacting to a future mandate under time pressure — it's already positioned ahead of one, should the requirement ever be extended to existing outlets, the same way it already has been for new ones.
